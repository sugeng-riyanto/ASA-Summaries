Dynamics — Explaining Motion

Newton's LawsF = maW = mgFree-Body DiagramsResultant ForcesTerminal Velocity
EXAMINER'S BRIEF

Kinematics describes motion; Dynamics explains it with forces. Golden rule: acceleration is always in the direction of the resultant force. Every full-marks dynamics answer begins with a correct labelled free-body diagram. Momentum, impulse and collisions are covered in Topic 6.

1 · Overview & Concept Map

A force is a push or pull that can change an object's shape or its motion. Forces are vectors; the combined effect of several forces is the resultant force. Aristotle claimed a force is needed to keep an object moving — wrong: Galileo showed that friction is the hidden force, and uniform motion is natural.

2 · Newton's Three Laws of Motion

1ST LAW · INERTIA

Every object continues in its state of rest, or of uniform velocity in a straight line, unless acted on by a resultant force.

Consequence: resultant force = 0 ⇒ a = 0 ⇒ stationary or constant velocity. An air-track glider floats on a cushion of air and moves at constant velocity because friction is absent.

2ND LAW · RATE OF CHANGE OF MOMENTUM

The resultant force on an object is equal to the rate of change of its momentum, in the direction of that change:

F = Δp / Δt  ⇒  for constant mass:  F = ma

3RD LAW · ACTION–REACTION

When object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. The two forces appear at the same time — neither causes the other.

3RD-LAW PAIR — THE 4 TESTS
  1. Act on two different objects (never the same object!)
  2. Equal in magnitude
  3. Opposite in direction
  4. Same type of force (both gravitational, both contact, both tension…)
THE CLASSIC TRAP

A book rests on a table: weight W (down) and normal contact force R (up) are equal & opposite, but they act on the same object (the book) — so they are NOT a 3rd-law pair. The true partner of the book's weight is the gravitational pull of the book on the Earth; the partner of R (table on book) is the book pushing down on the table.

3RD LAW IN ACTION

Walking: your foot presses backwards on the ground; the ground exerts an equal and opposite force on you, propelling you forwards. Rocket: engines push exhaust gases down; the gases push the rocket up.

3 · Mass, Inertia & Weight

DEFINITIONS

Mass (m): the property of an object that resists change in motion — a measure of inertia. Scalar, unit kg, the same everywhere.

Weight (W): the gravitational force on an object: W = mg. Vector (towards the centre of the Earth), unit N, depends on local g.

QuantitySymbolUnitBase unitsComment
massmkgkgscalar; constant everywhere; measures inertia
weightW = mgNkg m s⁻²a force; varies with gravitational field strength
g — TWO READINGS, ONE NUMBER

g = 9.81 m s⁻² (acceleration of free fall) ≡ g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹ (gravitational field strength = force per unit mass). The weight of a fixed mass depends on location: a 1 kg mass weighs 9.81 N on Earth but only 1.6 N on the Moon (gMoon = 1.6 N kg⁻¹).

MOON BUGGY

If a moon-buggy breaks down, it is no easier to get moving horizontally on the Moon than on Earth — its mass (inertia) is unchanged. But it is easier to lift, because its weight W = mg is smaller where g is smaller.

INERTIA DEMO — THE COIN TRICK

Flick a card from under a coin balanced on a glass: the coin drops straight in. The friction from the moving card is too small (and acts too briefly) to shift the coin sideways — its inertia keeps it at rest.

Measuring mass vs weight: a newton (spring) balance measures weight; a beam/lever balance compares masses (equal weights ⇒ equal masses, since W = mg).

4 · Newton's Second Law: F = ma

KEY EQUATION

F = m a

F = resultant force (N) · m = mass (kg) · a = acceleration (m s⁻²)

One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m s⁻² in the direction of the force.

THREE VITAL RULES
  1. F is the resultant force — add all forces as vectors first.
  2. Acceleration is always in the direction of the resultant force (a projectile accelerates downwards whatever its direction of travel).
  3. Use consistent SI units: kg, m, s, N, m s⁻².
SOLVED · FOUR STANDARDS

Train: resultant force F = 20 000 N on m = 10 000 kg → a = 2.0 m s⁻². Braking at a = −3.0 m s⁻² → F = 10 000 × (−3.0) = −30 000 N (opposite to motion).

Cyclist: rider 60 kg + bicycle 20 kg = 80 kg; driving force 200 N → a = 200/80 = 2.5 m s⁻².

Braking car: m = 500 kg, slows 20 → 0 m s⁻¹ in 10 s → a = −2.0 m s⁻²; F = 500 × (−2.0) = −1000 N.

Vehicle: m = 600 kg accelerates from rest to 8.0 m s⁻¹ in 20 s → a = 0.40 m s⁻²; F = 600 × 0.40 = 240 N.

LINK WITH KINEMATICS

Dynamics problems are usually two-stage: use suvat to find a, then F = ma for the force — or F = ma first, then suvat for distances and times. Always find a first. The bigger the mass, the smaller the acceleration for a given force (a = F/m) — a full rush-hour train accelerates more slowly than an empty one.

6 · Resultant Force & Equilibrium

TWO FORCES IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS

Resultant force,  F₁ − F₂ = ma

Adopt a sign convention (choose which direction is positive). The sign of the final answer gives the direction of the resultant force and hence the acceleration.

SOLVED · SHUTTLECOCK

R = √(8.0² + 6.0²) = √100 = 10 N, at θ = tan⁻¹(8/6) = 53° below the horizontal (a 3–4–5 triangle). The angle exceeds 45° because the downward force is the larger one.

EQUILIBRIUM & TRIANGLE OF FORCES

An object is in equilibrium when the resultant force is zero → it stays at rest or moves at constant velocity (1st law). For three coplanar forces in equilibrium, drawing the force arrows head-to-tail forms a closed triangle. Use it both ways: resultant = 0 ⇒ equilibrium; known equilibrium ⇒ forces add to zero (find unknowns).

SOLVED · PARACHUTIST

A parachutist weighs 1000 N; her open parachute pulls up with 2000 N. Resultant = 2000 − 1000 = 1000 N upwards. Since she is moving down, this upward resultant decelerates her until drag falls and a new, lower terminal velocity is reached.

7 · Trailers, Rockets, Lifts, Pulleys & Slopes

CAR & TRAILER

Car (M), trailer (m), tow-bar tension T, engine force F.

Car: F − T = Ma.  Trailer: T = ma.
Combined: F = (M + m)a.

ROCKET

Up positive: T − mg = ma ⇒ thrust T = m(g + a). Thrust must first overcome the weight for lift-off.

PULLEY (M > m)

Mg − T = Ma and T − mg = ma ⇒ (M − m)g = (M + m)a.

DOWN A SLOPE (angle θ)

Component of weight down slope = mg sin θ. mg sin θ − F = ma; N = mg cos θ. (N has no component down the slope — resolving along the slope avoids the unknown N.)

LIFT PROBLEMS · T − mg = ma (up positive)

constant velocity (either way)0T = mg
up & accelerating / down & decelerating+veT = m(g+a) > mg
up & decelerating / down & accelerating−veT = m(g−|a|) < mg
SOLVED · LIFT

Lift of total mass 650 kg moving down decelerates at 1.5 m s⁻². Moving down but slowing ⇒ a is + (opposite to v). T − mg = ma ⇒ T = 650 × 9.81 + 650 × 1.5 = 7400 N.

SOLVED · WATER SLIDE

Boy m = 40 kg on a 30° slope; friction up slope = 120 N. W = 40 × 9.81 = 392 N.

Component of W down slope = 392 sin 30° = 196 N; resultant = 196 − 120 = 76 N; a = 76/40 = 1.9 m s⁻².

8 · Friction, Drag & Terminal Velocity

A friction force acts along the common surface of contact, opposite the relative motion. The term viscous force (drag) describes friction in a fluid. Drag is zero when velocity is zero and increases with speed; it depends on shape, size and the fluid's viscosity.

Release: only weight acts → a = g (drag = 0 at the instant of release).
↓
Speeding up: drag grows with speed → resultant (W − drag) falls → a decreases.
↓
Terminal velocity: drag = weight → resultant = 0 → a = 0 → constant speed.
KEY RELATIONS

At any instant the resultant force is mg − F, so a = g − F/m. In a liquid with upthrust, terminal velocity is reached when weight = upthrust + viscous force (upthrust is negligible in air because air's density is low).

POWERED VEHICLES · TOP SPEED

For a vehicle on a level surface with engine force FE and combined resistive force FD: resultant = FE − FD, so a = (FE − FD)/m. Top speed is reached when driving force = drag; the engine's work is then dissipated as internal energy of the surroundings. A streamlined shape → less drag → higher top speed for the same engine power.

SOLVED · CAR WITH DRAG

Car m = 1200 kg, engine motive force 600 N. Initial a (drag = 0) = 600/1200 = 0.5 m s⁻². When drag = 400 N: resultant = 200 N → a = 200/1200 = 0.16 m s⁻².

ENERGY & SKYDIVING

At terminal velocity, the potential energy lost is converted to internal energy of the fluid by the drag force. A skydiver reaches ≈ 50 m s⁻¹; opening the parachute greatly increases drag → new, much lower terminal velocity for a safe landing. Insects survive falls because drag is large relative to their weight.

9 · Worked Examples & Exam Practice

EXAMPLE 1 · ROCKET

Rocket m = 550 kg blasts vertically from the launch pad at a = 4.2 m s⁻².

Weight = 550 × 9.81 = 5400 N. Thrust T = m(g + a) = 550 × (9.81 + 4.2) = 7700 N.

EXAMPLE 2 · BULLET INTO A TREE

Bullet m = 2.4 × 10⁻³ kg at 115 m s⁻¹ embeds itself 85 mm into a tree.

a = (v² − u²)/2s = (0 − 115²)/(2 × 0.085) ≈ −7.8 × 10⁴ m s⁻². Impact force F = ma = 2.4 × 10⁻³ × 7.8 × 10⁴ ≈ 190 N.

EXAMPLE 3 · LAWNMOWER

Lawnmower m = 18 kg pushed at constant speed by P = 80 N along a handle at 40° to the horizontal.

(a) Constant speed ⇒ resultant = 0 ⇒ friction F = P cos 40° = 80 × 0.766 = 61 N.

(b) To accelerate from rest to 1.2 m s⁻¹ in 2.0 s: a = 0.6 m s⁻²; required resultant = 18 × 0.6 = 10.8 N. So P cos 40° = 61 + 10.8 = 71.8 ⇒ P ≈ 94 N.

EXAMPLE 4 · BRICK ON A ROOF

Brick m = 3.2 kg on a 30° roof slides down with constant acceleration 2.0 m s⁻².

Component of W down slope = 3.2 × 9.81 × sin 30° = 15.7 N. Resultant = ma = 3.2 × 2.0 = 6.4 N. Friction F = 15.7 − 6.4 = 9.3 N (up the roof).

TRY YOURSELF (answers only)
  1. Force 5.0 N on mass 3.0 kg → a = 1.7 m s⁻².
  2. Stone 50 g from a catapult: rest to 8.0 m s⁻¹ over 30 cm → a = 107 m s⁻², average force ≈ 5.3 N.
  3. Rocket 5000 kg, resultant force 200 000 N → a = 40 m s⁻².
  4. Motorcyclist (60 kg + 40 kg bike), forward force 200 N, from rest for 5.0 s → v = 10 m s⁻¹.
  5. Car 800 kg accelerated from rest to 12 m s⁻¹ in 50 s → a = 0.24 m s⁻², F = 192 N.

MEMORY TRICKS
  • F = ma: "Force Makes Acceleration" — and they point the same way.
  • 3rd-law checklist: "Different objects, Equal, Opposite, Same type" (DEOS).
  • Terminal velocity: "drag grows until it equals weight → zero resultant → steady speed."
  • Slope trick: resolve along the slope to dodge the unknown N (N has no component down the slope).
  • Lift/trailer: isolate each body, write F = ma for each, then combine.
NEWTON'S LAWS

1st: rest/uniform v unless resultant F.
2nd: F = Δp/Δt → F = ma (const m).
3rd: equal & opposite on two different bodies.

11 · Deep Dive: The Anatomy of Newton's Laws

To master Dynamics, we must move beyond rote memorization and examine the precise anatomical definitions demanded by Cambridge examiners.

1ST LAW · INERTIA & FRAMES

The Law: An object remains at rest or in uniform velocity unless acted upon by a resultant external force.

Examiner Trap: Students often write "constant speed". This is incorrect. An object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating (changing direction), therefore a resultant force must be acting. The law strictly requires uniform velocity (constant speed in a straight line).

Inertia: Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia. It is the inherent resistance of an object to any change in its state of motion. It is not a force.

2ND LAW · THE MOMENTUM DERIVATION

The Law: The resultant force acting on an object is directly proportional to the rate of change of its momentum, and acts in the same direction.

Socratic Derivation: We often use F = ma. But where does this come from?

Fundamental Law: F ∝ Δp / Δt
↓
Substitute p = mv: F ∝ Δ(mv) / Δt
↓
If mass is constant: F ∝ m(Δv / Δt)
↓
Since a = Δv / Δt, we get F = ma
CRITICAL A-LEVEL BRIDGE

F = ma is a special case! It only applies when mass is constant. If a rocket burns fuel, or a conveyor belt accumulates sand, mass is changing. You must use the fundamental form: F = Δp / Δt. (We will explore variable mass systems extensively in Topic 6: Momentum).

3RD LAW · THE "DEOS" CHECKLIST

The Law: When two bodies interact, they exert forces on each other that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

To identify a true Newton's 3rd Law pair, apply the DEOS checklist:

  • Different objects (Act on body A and body B)
  • Equal in magnitude
  • Opposite in direction
  • Same type of force (e.g., both gravitational, both contact)
EXAMINER'S MYTH-BUSTER

The Myth: "A book rests on a table. Its Weight (down) and the Normal Reaction (up) are a Newton's 3rd Law pair because they are equal and opposite."

The Reality: FALSE. Both forces act on the same object (the book), and they are different types of force (Gravitational vs. Electromagnetic/Contact). They are balanced due to the 1st Law (Equilibrium), not the 3rd Law.

12 · Translational Equilibrium & Coplanar Forces

An object is in translational equilibrium if the resultant force acting on it is zero. This means the object is either stationary or moving with a constant velocity (Newton's 1st Law).

THE TRIANGLE OF FORCES

When three coplanar forces act on an object in equilibrium, they can be represented by the sides of a closed triangle, drawn head-to-tail in sequence.

Examiner Tip: If the triangle does not close, the forces are unbalanced, and the vector required to close the gap represents the resultant force.

RESOLVING FORCES (ORTHOGONAL METHOD)

For systems with more than three forces, the triangle method becomes impractical. Instead, resolve all forces into perpendicular (x and y) components.

  • Condition for Equilibrium: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0
  • Slope Trick: When an object rests on a slope of angle θ, it is mathematically elegant to resolve forces parallel and perpendicular to the slope, rather than strictly horizontal and vertical. This eliminates the unknown Normal force from the parallel equation.

13 · Bridge to Topic 4: Moments & Rotational Equilibrium

While translational equilibrium requires ΣF = 0, an object can still rotate if the forces act along different lines of action. To analyze rotation, we introduce the concept of the Moment of a Force.

MOMENT & TORQUE

Moment: The turning effect of a force about a pivot.

Moment = Force × perpendicular distance from the pivot

Unit: Newton-metre (N m). Note: Do not confuse with the Joule (J); moments are vectors (clockwise/anticlockwise), whereas energy is a scalar.

A Couple: Two forces that are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and do not share the same line of action.

Torque of a Couple = One of the forces × perpendicular distance between them

THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS

For an object to be in complete rotational equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point must equal the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that same point.

Strategic Pivot Selection: When solving statics problems, always choose your pivot at the point where an unknown force acts. Because the perpendicular distance to the pivot is zero, the moment of that unknown force is zero, effectively eliminating it from your equation.

COMPLETE EQUILIBRIUM CHECKLIST

Before declaring a rigid body in equilibrium in an exam, verify both conditions:

  1. Translational: Resultant force is zero (ΣF = 0).
  2. Rotational: Resultant moment is zero (ΣM = 0) about any point.
