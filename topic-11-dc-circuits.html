EXAMINER’S BRIEF

Two laws govern everything here. Kirchhoff I — charge is conserved (currents into a junction = currents out). Kirchhoff II — energy is conserved (Σ e.m.f.s = Σ p.d.s around any loop). From them we derive series/parallel resistance, the e.m.f. equation, divider rule and the potentiometer. Learn the two laws cold, then derive everything else from them.

2 · e.m.f. vs p.d. — the Energy Story

DEFINITIONS

e.m.f. (E) — the energy transferred per unit charge by the source in driving charge around the complete circuit. Unit: volt = J C⁻¹.

Potential difference (V) — the energy transferred per unit charge as charge passes through a component.

WHY IT MATTERS

A “1.5 V cell” delivers 1.5 J to each coulomb that passes through it. The charge then gives that energy away in the components. Kirchhoff II is this loop closed.

3 · Kirchhoff’s First Law — Conservation of Charge

KIRCHHOFF I

The sum of the currents entering any junction = the sum of the currents leaving that junction.

Σ Iin = Σ Iout

  • Charge cannot pile up or vanish at a point in a circuit.
  • If a billion electrons enter a point in 1 s, then a billion must leave it in 1 s.
  • At a simple junction: 5.0 A in → 2.0 A + 3.0 A out.
METER NOTE

An ammeter must be in series so the full current passes through it; a voltmeter must be in parallel so it reads p.d. without drawing much current.

4 · Kirchhoff’s Second Law — Conservation of Energy

KIRCH II

Around any closed loop: the sum of the e.m.f.s = the sum of the p.d.s.

Σ E = Σ IR

Think of moving 1 C around the loop: it gains E joules at each source of e.m.f. and loses V J at each component. Returning to its start, energy must be the same — otherwise we'd create energy from nothing.

SIGN CONVENTIONS

e.m.f.s: a battery whose “push” (conventional current) travels with your chosen loop direction → positive; against → negative.

p.d.s: a component carrying current with your loop direction → positive IR; against → negative IR.

Wrong direction guess? The algebra simply returns a negative current — the actual direction is opposite to your arrow.

WORKED EXAMPLE · TWO LOOPS

Upper loop: 6.0 = 30I₃ + 10I₁ · Lower loop: 2.0 = 30I₃
From lower: I₃ = 0.067 A. In upper: I₁ = (6 − 2)/10 = 0.400 A.
Junction: I₁ = I₂ + I₃ → I₂ = 0.067 − 0.400 = −0.33 A (flows opposite to the arrow).

COMMON ERROR

The outermost loop of a multi-loop circuit often gives a dependent equation (it's the difference/sum of two inner loops). Don't count it — you'll just solve zero equations too few.

6 · Series Resistance — Derived, Not Memorised

Consider resistors in series: the same current I passes through each (Kirchhoff I), and the supply p.d. is shared.

DERIVATION

V = V₁ + V₂  (Kirchhoff II around the single loop)

IR = IR₁ + IR₂

Cancel common I:   R = R₁ + R₂ + …

"Two identical in series double the resistance." Applying Kirchhoff I first: same current; K II: add p.d.s → combined resistance adds.

7 · Parallel Resistance — Derivation

In parallel, the current divides but every branch sees the same p.d..

DERIVATION

I = I₁ + I₂  (Kirchhoff I at junction)

I₁R₁ = I₂R₂ = V  (Kirchhoff II: same p.d. across branches)

V/R = V/R₁ + V/R₂ →  1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …

  • Combined resistance always less than the smallest branch (two equal Rs → R/2).
  • A shorted branch (R ≈ 0) shorts the whole parallel block.
TWO-BULB TRICK

Two identical resistors in parallel → combined R/2 — a handy mental double-check in exams.

8 · Internal Resistance & the Terminal p.d.

A real battery behaves like an ideal e.m.f. E in series with a small internal resistance r. The energy lost in r shows up as heating inside the battery.

KEY EQUATIONS

E = IR + Ir = I(R + r)

V = E − Ir (terminal p.d. = e.m.f. − drop across r)

The terminal p.d. V is what a voltmeter across the battery actually reads. Since V = IR:

V is largest at no load, and falls as the load current rises

  • Short-circuit: R → 0 → I ≈ E/r (huge; battery overheats fast).
  • With a digital voltmeter (R ≈ 10⁷ Ω) the tiny current makes Ir negligible → it reads ≈ E.
THE V–I GRAPH

V = E − Ir is y = mx + c with y = V, x = I.

Gradient = −r (negative because V falls as I grows); intercept on the V-axis = E. Experiment: change the load, record (V, I), plot and read off E and r.

REAL-LIFE CHEW

Headlamps on while starting a car — the starter draws a big current, terminal p.d. drops, the headlamps dim. That's V = E − Ir in the flesh.

9 · Potential Dividers

Two steps are needed. First, why the shape: pot = “the voltage is apportioned”. A pair of series resistors splits the supply voltage in the ratio of their resistances.

THE POTENTIAL DIVIDER RULE

Vout = Vin · R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)

Vout is the voltage across the resistance taken over = R₂; R₁ is the other resistor; Vin across the pair.

Mixing in a variable resistor, the slider lets Vout range from 0 (slider at bottom) up to Vin (slider at top).

2 resistors in series, 6.0 V total
▼
each takes a share = (its R / total R) × V_total
▼
R₂ = R₁ → exactly half the e.m.f.
TRAP

Vout is across R₂ — the resistor sharing the divider with the component feeding the meter. Reverse which one you label and the ratio flips. Draw the circuit, label the measured resistor R₂, write the rule once.

10 · Sensors: Thermistor & Light-Dependent Resistor

A thermistor or LDR only changes resistance — but a voltmeter needs a voltage. So you turn the resistance change into a voltage change by wiring the sensor into a potential divider against a fixed resistor.

SensorBehaviourOutput when it sits on top
LDRR falls as light increasesV out falls as light rises
NTC thermistorR falls as temperature rises (semiconductor: more free e⁻)V out falls as temperature rises
LDR CIRCUIT

Light ↑ → R(LDR) ↓ → the divider reshares: V across the fixed R rises, V across the LDR falls. The output follows the light hitting the LDR.

THERMISTOR CIRCUIT

Temperature ↑ → R(thermistor) falls → the divider re-shares. Read the other resistor to flip the direction — outputs can be rising or falling with temperature.

SOLVED · 60 lux LDR

At 60 lux an LDR has R = 20 kΩ; fixed R = 3.0 kΩ, Vin = 10 V.
Vout across the fixed resistor = 10 × 3.0/(3.0 + 20) = 1.30 V (don't write 8.69 — that's the voltage across the LDR!). Give final answers to the sensible s.f.

WHY METAL WIRE ≈ POOR

A wire sensor changes small and linearly; a thermistor changes a lot (exponentially) with temperature — much more useful, though must be calibrated.

11 · Potentiometer & Null Methods

WHAT IT DOES

A potentiometer is a 1 m uniform resistance wire used as a comparing device — not to burn a current, but to find a balance (null) point. Potential dividers can't do this.

  • Driver cell across A–B sets a known p.d. gradient along the wire (E₀ = 2.0 V → 2.0 V/A end, 0 V/B end).
  • The cell X under test is connected positive-to-A; a galvanometer + jockey slides along the wire.
  • At balance Y, galvanometer reads zero → p.d. across AY = e.m.f. of X. No current flows from X → its terminal p.d. = its true e.m.f. (unlike a voltmeter).
BALANCE EQUATIONS

EX = E₀ · AY / AB  (compare with driver)

EX/EY = AC/AD  (compare two cells)

DOCTOR'S NOTE

Touch gently, never slide the jockey — scraping the wire makes it non-uniform, so the voltage stops being linear. Never skid the sharp edge along the wire.

NULL METHOD

You're looking for zero current — the reader is exact and doesn't disturb the circuit. The galvanometer's role is to detect the balance, not to read currents.

12 · Worked Examples & Revision

Worked example — e.m.f./internal resistance

W.E. · FINDING r

battery E = 6.0 V drives 0.40 A into a 13.5 Ω resistor.
E = I(R + r) → 6.0 = 0.40(13.5 + r) → 15.0 = 13.5 + r → r = 1.5 Ω.

Worked example — the potentiometer balance

W.E. · STANDARD CELL

Driver 4.0 V across 1.00 m. Your unknown cell gives balance at 37 cm; a standard 1.230 V gives 31.2 cm.
a p.d. per cm = 4.0/100 = 0.040 V cm⁻¹.
b E ≈ 0.040 × 37 = 1.48 V (estimate: driver-cell voltage unknown, wire assumption).
c E = 1.230 × (37.0/31.2) = 1.46 V.

Past-paper style Q&A

PAST-PAPER STYLE

Q1. 6.0 Ω ∥ 3.0 Ω → 2.0 Ω; battery E 4.5 V, r 0.50 Ω → I = 4.5/2.5 = 1.8 A (answer B).
Q2. Temperature decreases → thermistor R rises → its share of V rises → V across the other resistor falls.
Q3. Two 6 V batteries in parallel give 6 V — the same p.d. forces each cell to drive the same work, and charge is conserved; in anti-series they cancel (0 V).
