EXAMINER'S BRIEF

This topic links the microscopic structure of matter to its macroscopic behaviour under load. Examiners reward precise definitions of stress, strain and Young modulus, correct reading of force–extension and stress–strain graphs, and a clearly described Young-modulus experiment. Remember: strain has no unit — a favourite mark-scheme point.

1 · Overview & Concept Map

Matter and materials asks: how does a material respond when forces try to stretch or squash it? We move from the particle picture of the three states of matter, through Hooke's law, to the stiffness measure known as the Young modulus, and finish with the energy stored in a deformed solid.

WHY IT MATTERS

In a solid, strong intermolecular bonds hold particles in a lattice. When a tensile force stretches the solid, these bonds stretch like tiny springs — this is why solids obey Hooke's law for small extensions, and why they snap if stretched too far.

3 · Tensile & Compressive Forces

A pair of forces is needed to change an object's shape (a single force would just accelerate it).

TWO TYPES OF DEFORMATION

Tensile deformation: two equal & opposite forces pull outwards → object stretches (length increases).

Compressive deformation: two equal & opposite forces push inwards → object is squeezed (length decreases).

BENDING

When a wire or beam bends, the outer surface is in tension (stretched) while the inner surface is in compression (squashed). The middle layer is roughly unchanged.

4 · Hooke's Law & Elastic Behaviour

HOOKES LAW

F = kx

extension x ∝ applied load F, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

k = F/x is the spring (force) constant, unit N m⁻¹.

A stiffer spring has a larger k. Hooke's law applies to springs, wires and any material within its linear region — not just springs.

ELASTIC DEFORMATION

Object returns to original shape when the load is removed. Occurs up to the elastic limit. Bonds stretch but do not break.

PLASTIC DEFORMATION

Object is permanently deformed; it does not return to its original shape. Occurs beyond the elastic limit. Particles slide past each other.

KEY DISTINCTIONS
  • Limit of proportionality (P): point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to force (graph stops being straight).
  • Elastic limit (E): point beyond which the material is permanently deformed. P and E are close, but not the same point.

5 · Stress, Strain & Young Modulus

The spring constant k describes one particular object. To compare different materials, independent of size or shape, we use stress, strain and the Young modulus.

STRESS

stress σ = F / A

force per unit cross-sectional area · unit Pa (N m⁻²)

STRAIN

strain ε = x / L

extension per unit original length · strain is a ratio — it has NO unit

YOUNG MODULUS

E = stress / strain = (F/A) / (x/L) = FL / (Ax)

unit Pa (N m⁻²) — same as stress, since strain is dimensionless

WHY LONG & THIN?

A long wire gives a larger, more measurable extension for a given load. A thin wire gives a larger stress for a given load. Both improve the accuracy of the Young-modulus experiment.

MaterialYoung modulus / GPa
steel210
copper130
aluminium70
glass70–80
rubber0.01

Remember: 1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa. Steel is far stiffer than rubber — a much larger stress is needed to produce the same strain.

6 · Stress–Strain Graphs

Plotting stress against strain (instead of force against extension) gives a graph whose gradient is the Young modulus, independent of the specimen's dimensions.

The initial straight section obeys Hooke's law. Its gradient = Young modulus E. Only this linear part may be used to find E.

Comparing material behaviour

BehaviourGraph featureExample
BrittleSteep straight line; fractures suddenly with little/no plastic deformationglass, cast iron
DuctileLinear then a long plastic region; can be drawn into a wirecopper, mild steel
PolymericNon-linear S-shape; very large strain before breakingrubber, nylon

7 · Experiment: Young Modulus of a Wire

PROCEDURE
  1. Clamp a long, thin wire at the top and hang weights from the free end.
  2. Measure the original length L from the clamp to a reference marker, using a metre rule.
  3. Measure the diameter d with a micrometer screw gauge at several places along the wire; average. Then A = πd²/4.
  4. Add loads in steps, recording the extension x each time from the marker and rule.
  5. Calculate stress = F/A and strain = x/L for each load.
  6. Plot stress (y-axis) against strain (x-axis). The gradient of the straight section = E.
  7. Alternative: plot F against x; gradient = F/x = EA/L, so E = gradient × L/A.
  8. Unload gradually to confirm the wire has not been permanently stretched.
ACCURACY POINTS (Paper 5)
  • Long wire → larger, more measurable extension.
  • Measure diameter at several points → reduces error from non-uniform wire.
  • Use a micrometer (±0.01 mm), not a ruler, for the diameter.
  • Wear eye protection; do not overload the wire (it can snap).
  • Draw a best-fit line; find E from the gradient of the linear region only.

8 · Elastic Potential Energy (Strain Energy)

Stretching a material requires work. This work is stored as elastic (strain) potential energy, recoverable if the elastic limit is not exceeded.

DERIVATION · ½Fx = ½kx²

Within the linear region, force rises from 0 to F as extension grows from 0 to x. Work done = average force × extension:

Eₚ = ½ F x

Since Hooke's law gives F = kx, substituting:

Eₚ = ½ k x²

Energy in joules if k in N m⁻¹ and x in metres.

IMPORTANT

The ½Fx and ½kx² formulas apply only within the linear (Hookean) region. Beyond the limit of proportionality the graph curves, and you must find the area by counting squares instead.

9 · Worked Examples

EXAMPLE 1 · SPRING CONSTANT

An elastic cord has original length 25 cm. With a force of 0.75 N its length becomes 40 cm.

Extension x = 40 − 25 = 15 cm = 0.15 m.

k = F/x = 0.75 / 0.15 = 5.0 N m⁻¹

EXAMPLE 2 · YOUNG MODULUS OF STEEL

A steel wire of length 200.0 cm and cross-sectional area 0.50 mm² is stretched by a force of 50 N; its new length is 200.1 cm.

Extension x = 200.1 − 200.0 = 0.1 cm = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m. L = 2.00 m. A = 0.50 × 10⁻⁶ m².

stress = F/A = 50 / (0.50 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁸ Pa

strain = x/L = 1.0 × 10⁻³ / 2.00 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵

E = stress/strain = 1.0 × 10⁸ / 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa

EXAMPLE 3 · EXTENSION OF A COPPER WIRE

A copper wire of length 1.00 m and diameter 1.00 mm is stretched by a tensile force of 10 N. E = 130 GPa.

A = πd²/4 = π(1.0 × 10⁻³)²/4 = 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ m².

Rearrange E = FL/(Ax) → x = FL/(AE) = 10 × 1.00 / (7.85 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.30 × 10¹¹)

x ≈ 9.8 × 10⁻⁵ m ≈ 0.098 mm

EXAMPLE 4 · STRAIN ENERGY

A spring of spring constant 65 N m⁻¹ is extended by 1.2 cm within its limit of proportionality.

Eₚ = ½kx² = ½ × 65 × (1.2 × 10⁻²)² = 4.7 × 10⁻³ J

EXAMPLE 5 · FISHING LINE

A fishing line of length 1.0 m and radius 0.25 mm has extension 50 mm under a load; the stress is 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa.

A = πr² = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m².

Load F = stress × A = 2.0 × 10⁸ × 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ ≈ 39 N

strain = x/L = 0.050/1.0 = 0.050

E = stress/strain = 2.0 × 10⁸ / 0.050 = 4.0 × 10⁹ Pa

If the extension uncertainty is ±1 mm, percentage uncertainty in strain = (1/50) × 100 = 2%, so absolute uncertainty in E ≈ 2% of 4.0 × 10⁹ = 0.08 × 10⁹ Pa.

10 · Mistakes · Tips · Tricks · FAQ

✗ Common Mistake✓ Correction
Giving strain a unitStrain is a ratio (x/L) — it has no unit.
Using diameter instead of radius in A = πr²A = πr² = πd²/4. Divide diameter by 2 first.
Using mm² directly for areaConvert: 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m².
Using ½kx² beyond the elastic limit½kx² applies only in the linear (Hookean) region.
Confusing limit of proportionality with elastic limitP = where graph stops being straight; E = where permanent deformation begins. Close, but distinct.
Finding E from the curved part of the graphUse only the straight (linear) section; its gradient = E.
MEMORY TRICKS
  • Stress = Force/Area (F over A). Strain = extension/Length (x over L).
  • Young modulus = stress/strain — "stiffness of the material".
  • Steel stiff, rubber soft: bigger E → stiffer material.
  • Strain energy = area under F–x graph = ½Fx = ½kx².
  • Brittle breaks; ductile draws; polymeric stretches a lot.
FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q1. Why is strain dimensionless? → It is a ratio of two lengths (x/L); the units cancel.

Q2. Why use a long thin wire for the Young-modulus experiment? → Long → bigger extension (easier to measure); thin → bigger stress for a given load.

Q3. Difference between limit of proportionality and elastic limit? → P: graph stops being straight (Hooke's law ends). E: permanent deformation begins.

Q4. Why is steel stiffer than rubber? → Steel has a far larger Young modulus, so a much larger stress is needed for the same strain.

Q5. Does the Young modulus depend on the wire's size? → No. E is a material property; stress and strain remove the dependence on dimensions.

11 · One-Page Revision

HOOKES LAW

F = kx
k = F/x (N m⁻¹)
Valid up to limit of proportionality
Elastic: returns · Plastic: permanent

STRESS & STRAIN

stress σ = F/A (Pa)
strain ε = x/L (no unit)
E = stress/strain = FL/(Ax)

STRAIN ENERGY

Eₚ = ½Fx = ½kx²
= area under force–extension graph
(linear region only)

MATERIAL BEHAVIOUR

Brittle: snaps suddenly (glass)
Ductile: large plastic region (copper)
Polymeric: large strain (rubber)

YOUNG MODULUS EXPERIMENT

Clamp long thin wire · measure L (metre rule) & d (micrometer, average, A = πd²/4) · add loads · record extensions · plot stress vs strain · gradient = E.

KEY VALUES & UNITS

1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa · 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m² · E(steel) ≈ 2.1 × 10¹¹ Pa · E(copper) ≈ 1.3 × 10¹¹ Pa · E(rubber) ≈ 10⁷ Pa · strain is dimensionless.

Stress stretches, strain records it, and E tells you how stiff it is. ✔
