Electricity — Current, p.d. & Resistance

Q = ItI = nAvep.d. vs e.m.f.R = V/IOhm’s lawI–V curvesρ = RA/LP = VI
EXAMINER’S BRIEF

Three ideas rule AS electricity: charge flows (Q = It), charge carriers drift (I = nAve), and energy per charge defines p.d. and e.m.f. Exam favourites: the nAve triad question, p.d. ↔ e.m.f. definitions, and sketch-the-I–V-curve tasks.

1 · Charge & the Coulomb

KEY EQUATION

ΔQ = I Δt

charge flowing past a point = current × time · 1 C = 1 A s

  • Current is measured in amperes (A); charge in coulombs (C).
  • 1 A = 1 C flowing past a point each second.
  • Charge is quantised: it comes in integer multiples of the elementary charge e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (electron −e, proton +e). A charge like 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is impossible (2.5 e).
  • Illustrative count: it takes ~6 × 10¹⁸ electrons to make 1 C.
SOLVED · Q = It

Current of 0.40 A for 15 s → Q = 0.40 × 15 = 6.0 C.  Give Q = 150 C in t = 30 s → I = 150/30 = 5.0 A.

SOLVED · CAR BATTERY

Battery rated “50 A h” → Q = 50 × 3600 = 1.8 × 10⁵ C. Also: a lamp 10 A for 1.0 h → Q = 10 × 3600 = 3.6 × 10⁴ C.

TWIN TRAPS

Always convert hours → seconds (ΔQ = IΔt uses seconds). Reading “A h” as charge directly is a shortcut — 1 A h = 3600 C.

2 · Electric Current & Conventional Flow

DEFINITION

Current is the rate of flow of charge past a point (I = ΔQ/Δt). Its unit is the ampere.

  • In a metal, current is the flow of conduction (free) electrons drifting among fixed positive ions.
  • Conventional current is defined from + to −; electrons (negative) drift − to +, the opposite way. (Tradition: chosen before electrons were discovered.)
  • In an electrolyte both positive and negative ions move; in a beam only protons/positive ions may flow — all are charge carriers.
  • The whole circuit takes current almost instantly: an electric field propagates fast, even though the electrons drift slowly — the water-pipe analogy!
SOLVED · ELECTRON COUNT

Current of 4.5 mA for 3 min → Q = 4.5 × 10⁻³ × 180 = 0.81 C. Number of electrons = Q/e = 0.81/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.1 × 10¹⁸ electrons.

3 · Current & Mean Drift Velocity

KEY EQUATION

I = n A v q

n = number of charge carriers per m³ · A = cross-sectional area (m²) · v = mean drift velocity (m s⁻¹) · q = charge per carrier (C)

  • n (number density) for copper ≈ 10²⁸–10²⁹ m⁻³; semiconductors ~10²³ m⁻³.
  • Drift velocity is the average velocity of carriers along the wire (random collisions!). Typical copper v ≈ 0.1 mm s⁻¹ even though electron speed between collisions ~10⁵ m s⁻¹.
  • Why so slow yet instantaneous current? The pulse arrives at once — the electrons dribble along.
  • Narrowing A for fixed I → v rises; low n → v rises; larger I → v rises: v ∝ I/Anq.
SOLVED · DRIFT VELOCITY

Copper wire A = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ m², I = 1.0 A, n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³: v = I/(nAe) = 1.0 / (8.5×10²⁸ × 5.0×10⁻⁶ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹) = 1.0/0.068 = 15 × 10⁻⁶ m s⁻¹ (~1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹) — barely a ripple!

HOMOGENEITY CHECKunits: m⁻³ × m² × m s⁻¹ × A s = A ✓

4 · Potential Difference vs e.m.f.

DEFINITIONS

Potential difference (V) across two points = energy transferred per unit charge as charge flows through a component (energy is lost/converted).

e.m.f. (E) of a source = energy supplied per unit charge in driving charge completely round the circuit (energy is gained from non-electric source).

V = W / Q   ·   [V] = J C⁻¹   ·   E = W / Q

  • 1 V = 1 J of energy per 1 C of charge.
  • The name “electromotive force” is a bad legacy — it’s an energy-per-charge idea, not a force!
  • In a closed circuit lacking internal resistance: E total = Σ V drops.
  • Electron-volt connection: an electron accelerated through 50 kV gains energy W = eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 50 000 = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ J.
EXAM TIPQuestion “p.d. vs e.m.f.” → always answer with “energy per unit charge; p.d. is energy converted in a component, e.m.f. is energy delivered by a source around the circuit.”

5 · Resistance & Ohm’s Law

DEFINITION & UNIT

Resistance R = V / I , unit the ohm (Ω) = 1 V A⁻¹.

R = V / I

  • A component obeys Ohm’s law when current ∝ potential difference across its ends at a stated constant physical condition (e.g. constant temperature).
  • Ohm’s law ≠ “R = V/I” — every device has a ratio, but only ohmic ones keep V∝I (constant R).
  • Metals at fixed temperature follow Ohm’s law → straight I–V line through origin.
SOLVED · THE TRIPLE

V = IR applies in three guises:
Lamp 3.0 V with R = 15 Ω → I = 0.20 A.
Motor I=1.0 A & 50 Ω → V = 50 V; doubled current → 100 V (fixed R).
Headlamp R = 36 Ω at 12 V → I = 0.33 A.
Bathroom heater: P = 1.0 kW at 230 V → R = V²/P = 230²/1000 = 53 Ω.

INSTRUMENT CONNECTION Ammeter in series (same current); voltmeter in parallel across component (measures V). Check this standard circuit diagram every year — likely a drawing question.

7 · Thermistor & Light-Dependent Resistor

NTC THERMISTOR

resistance ↓ as temperature ↑

Metal-oxide device. At room ~kΩ → few tens of Ω at 100 °C. Used in water-temperature sensors, car-ice alerts, baby breathing monitors, fire alarms.

LDR

resistance ↓ as light intensity ↑

High-resistivity semiconductor; light frees carriers → conducts. Sunlight ~those few hundred Ω, darkness ~millions of Ω. Use in light-controlled switching.

CONFUSION GUARD Thermistor vs LDR: heating → lower R (NTC), brighter light → lower R (LDR). They look the same on a resistance-vs-driving-axis sketch. In series with a fixed resistor they form a potential divider (Ch 11).

8 · Resistivity & ρ = RA/L

KEY EQUATION

R = ρ L / A

ρ = L-atT resistivity (unit Ω m), L = length (m), A = cross-sectional area (m²)

Geometrical logic: 2 wires in series, doubling L, doubles R & 2 wires in parallel with same A gives half R — matches halving the cross-sectional area in series circuit model.

SOLVED · EUREKA WIRE

L = 2.6 m, A = 2.5×10⁻⁷ m², ρₑᵤ = 49×10⁻⁸ Ω m → R = 49×10⁻⁸ × 2.6 / 2.5×10⁻⁷ = 5.1 Ω.

SOLVED · COPPER SUCCESSIVE

1.0 m of 1.0 mm² area at ~1.7×10⁻⁸: R ≈ 1.7×10⁻⁸ × 1 / 10⁻⁶ = 0.017 Ω. ⚠ A thin, long manganin heater coil needs m, with area sometimes given as mm² → convert (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²).

ORIGIN OF RESISTANCE Trend in metals: higher temp → more lattice vibration → more collisions → greater R (small current in earlier graphs). Resistance rises with temp; resistivity also rises with temp in metals.

9 · Electrical Power & Energy

POWER TRIO

P = V I  ·  P = I²R  ·  P = V²/R

energy transferred: W = V I t [J, or kWh in billing]

  • W (energy) not to be confused with W (watts).
  • Power distribution: transmission at HIGH V, LOW I → small I²R losses (P ∝ I²).
  • 1 kWh = 1000 × 3600 = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
SOLVED · POWER-PLAN EXAMPLES

a) power station 20 MW at 200 kV → I = P/V = 20×10⁶/200×10³ = 100 A.

b) cable 15 km of 0.20 Ω/km → R = 3.0 Ω → P_waste = I²R = (100)² × 3.0 = 3×10⁴ W (30 kW), only 0.15% of output.

SOLVED · BATHROOM HEATER

230 V, 1.0 kW → R = V²/P = 230²/1000 = 52.9 Ω ≈ 53 Ω. Hall-bulbs: “60 W vs 100 W” → smaller V² of the 60 W bulb means higher resistance.

FUSE CHOICE For a 120 V, 450 W device → I = 450/120 = 3.75 A. Choose the next-highest safety fuse: 5 A — just above the operating current so it never melt under normal use, yet low enough to protect the circuit.

MEMORY TRICKS
  • Q = It — clean coulombs from amps & seconds.
  • I = nAve — “nAve the drift!” (number-density × Area × velocity × charge).
  • Resistivity trick: ρ is measured in Ω m, so that’s R × A ÷ L.
  • Filament curve = “it bends downward, showing resistance rising as it warms”.
  • Power: choose the formula that keeps knowns — V I when you have both; I²R when using current; V²/R when e.m.f. known.
