Motion in a Circle

Radiansω & v = rωCentripetal forcea = v²/rBanked curvesVertical circlesConical pendulum
EXAMINER’S BRIEF

Circular motion feels counter-intuitive because speed is constant but velocity is not. The whole topic sits on one idea: any object going round a circle has a resultant force pointing to the centre (centripetal) at 90° to its velocity. That force does no work — so speed never changes, only direction does. Master ω = 2π/T, v = rω, a = v²/r and F = mv²/r, then learn where each real force (friction, tension, normal contact, gravity) plays the centripetal role.

1 · Radians & Angular Displacement

THE RADIAN

1 radian = the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius.

θ = s / r   (arc length ÷ radius)

s and r are both metres, so a radian is a dimensionless ratio — just a pure number with a name (rad).

CHANGE

60° → 60 × π/180 = π/3 rad. 0.75 rad → 0.75 × 180/π ≈ 43°. Doubling both the arc and the radius leaves θ exactly unchanged — the angle is a ratio, independent of circle size.

The angular displacement θ of a particle is the angle through which a radius line has turned from a fixed starting direction — the circular-motion twin of linear displacement s.

2 · Angular Speed & v = rω

Angular speed ω (omega) = angle swept per second, unit rad s⁻¹. Angular velocity = angular speed in a stated direction (clockwise / anticlockwise).

DEFINITIONS

ω = Δθ / Δt   ·   ω = 2π / T

T = period (time for one revolution); one turn = 2π rad.

Δθ = Δs/r (definition of the radian)
▼
divide both sides by Δt → Δθ/Δt = (Δs/Δt)/r
▼
ω = Δθ/Δt and v = Δs/Δt, hence
▼
v = r ω
WHOLE DISC, ONE ω

Every point on a spinning disc has the same ω but a different v — the tip of a hand (r large) moves fastest, points near the axle crawl. ω belongs to the whole rotation; v belongs to one chosen radius.

WORKED · SPIN DRYER

Drum radius 0.50 m, 720 rpm → 12 rev s⁻¹. ω = 2π × 12 = 75 rad s⁻¹. Linear speed v = rω = 0.50 × 75 = 38 m s⁻¹. Centripetal acceleration a = v²/r = 38²/0.5 ≈ 2800 m s⁻² — 284 g!

REVOLUTIONS → ω

Convert rpm to rev s⁻¹ first, then multiply by 2π. 1200 rpm → 20 s⁻¹ → ω = 126 rad s⁻¹. Watch units: angles must be in radians, not degrees, before using v = rω.

3 · Steady Speed, Changing Velocity

An object rounding a circle at constant speed is not in equilibrium — its velocity is a vector and the direction is turning all the time.

KEY IDEA

Speed is constant (scalar, magnitude only). Velocity is continuously changing (direction of the tangent rotates). A change in velocity is an acceleration — so circular motion is acceleration without speeding up.

Newton’s first law: without a resultant force the object travels in a straight line
▼
curve appears ⇒ a force must be bending the path
▼
that resultant force is perpendicular to the motion and always points to the centre — the centripetal force
TANGENT ESCAPE

Let go of the string and the bung flies off along the tangent — not “outwards radiating”. Same for a car that skids off an ice bend: it carries on in a straight line because the tyres can no longer supply the centripetal friction.

4 · Centripetal Acceleration

Two velocity vectors a tiny time apart differ by a small Δv that (for a tiny arc) points straight to the centre — always at a right angle to the velocity. That Δv per Δt is the acceleration: constant in magnitude, continuously redirecting the motion around the circle.

KEY EQUATIONS

a = v²/r  =  rω²     F = mv²/r  =  mrω²

direction: towards the centre of the circle, at right angles to the velocity

similar triangles: Δv/v = Δs/r
▼
Δv = v·Δs/r, divide by Δt → Δv/Δt = (Δs/Δt)(v/r)
▼
a = Δv/Δt and v = Δs/Δt ⇒ a = v²/r
▼
substitute v = rω ⇒ a = rω²
NO WORK IS DONE

The force is at 90 to the displacement, so W = Fs cos 90° = 0. No work → no energy change → speed constant. The centripetal force only redirects the motion, never feeds energy into it. If the force has any component along v, the speed would change — exactly what happens for non-uniform circles.

CHECK THE NUMBERS

ISS at 340 km, a = 8.8 m s⁻², r = 6.74×10⁶ m: v = √(ar) = √(8.8×6.74e6) ≈ 7700 m s⁻¹; T = 2πr/v ≈ 5500 s ≈ 1.5 h per orbit.

ORBITAL SPEED

If gravity alone provides the force: mv²/r = mg → v = √(gr). Near Earth g = 9.81, r = 6.4×10⁶ m → v ≈ 7.9 km s⁻¹ — Newton’s cannonball speed from the thought experiment.

5 · Centripetal Force

The centripetal force is not a new force — it is the name we give to whatever real force that pulls towards the centre.

SituationForce providing the centripetal force
planet orbits the Sungravitational attraction (Sun’s pull)
Moon orbits EarthEarth’s gravity
car on a flat bendfriction (tyres → road)
ball on a stringtension in the string
electron in a magnetic fieldmagnetic force (topic 22)
fairground wall-of-deathnormal contact from the wall
lazy susan / turntable objectstatic friction
FEELING “THROWN OUTWARDS”

It is inertia, not a force: your body wants to keep going straight. The seat supplies a centripetal force — typically friction beneath the centre of mass — to turn you with the car, so your upper body seems to fling outwards. There is no real outward force on your free-body diagram.

CHANGE THE FORM

Use F = mv²/r when you know the speed; F = mrω² when you know the rotation. They are the same quantity — pick whichever the question feeds. At the same v, a bigger r needs less force; at the same ω, a bigger r needs more force (F ∝ r).

6 · Where the Force Comes From

Car on a level bend

Vertical: normal N balances weight mg (no vertical acceleration). Horizontal: static friction F between tyres and road is the unbalanced centripetal force.

MAX CORNERING SPEED

mv²/r ≤ μN = μmg ⇒ v_max = √(μg r)

On ice or oil, friction drops → v_max plummets → the car skids straight on.

Aircraft banking

When an aircraft banks, lift provides the centripetal force. The lift L is tilted: its vertical component balances the weight, its horizontal component supplies the turn force.

ANGLE OF BANK

tan θ = v² / (r·g)

WHY BANKING HELPS

Banking lets the road/air provide part of the turn force without demanding extra friction — safer and more comfortable. Too slow on the bank → slide down the slope; too fast → slide up the slope.

7 · Banked Tracks & Aircraft

BANKED TRACK

Vertical: Ncosθ = mg  ·  horizontal: Nsinθ = mv²/r

tanθ = v²/(rg)

θ independent of mass — bikers, cars, trucks all take the ideal bank at the same speed.

TOO SLOW / TOO FAST

Too slow → the car wants to slide downhill; friction acts up the slope to hold it. Too fast → wants to slide uphill; friction reverses. Friction + banking together give a range of safe speeds.

AIRCRAFT BANK

Aircraft: lift L replaced N. Vertical L cosθ = mg, horizontal L sinθ = mv²/r → same tanθ = v²/(rg). Diving-plane “pull out” is just the bottom half of a vertical circle: N − mg = mv²/r.

8 · Vertical Circles

At every point the centripetal force is the resultant toward the centre. The weight keeps pointing straight down, so the normal contact force has to vary around the loop to keep the resultant on target.

AT THE BOTTOM

Centripetal force = N − mg (normal up, weight down)
Nbottom = mv²/r + mg — the largest contact force in the loop, and why rides feel heaviest at the bottom.

AT THE TOP

Centripetal force = N + mg (both act down, into the circle!)
Ntop = mv²/r − mg — lighter at the top.

THE CRITICAL TOP SPEED

At the top, if v is too small, N would have to go negative — impossible! The minimum speed that keeps contact has N → 0:

mg = mv²/r  ⇒  vcritical = √(gr)

Slower than this and the bucket empties / the rider leaves the seat.

WORKED · ROLLERCOASTER LOOP

Loop diameter 8 m ⇒ r = 4 m, at the top N = 0: v = √(gr) = √(9.8×4) = 6.3 m s⁻¹.

STRING VS ROD

A string can only pull, so a ball on a string must meet v ≥ √(gr) at the top. A rigid rod can block pull, so N may push — the ball can crawl over the top at any speed.

9 · The Conical Pendulum

A stone swept in a horizontal circle while the string leans at an angle θ to the vertical — an ice-cream cone shape. The two components of the tension T share the load:

CONICAL PENDULUM EQUATIONS

T cosθ = mg   (vertical balance)
T sinθ = mv²/r   (horizontal centripetal)

tanθ = v²/(rg) = rω²/g

WHY THE HORIZONTAL STRING IS IMPOSSIBLE

If the string were horizontal, T would have no vertical component to balance mg — impossible. The string must slope down under the weight, giving T cosθ = mg.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Bung of mass 0.20 kg swinging with the string at 35° to the vertical. Vertical balance: T cos35° = mg → T = 0.20 × 9.81 / cos35° ≈ 2.4 N. Then T sin35° = mv²/r fixes v, and finally ω = v/r. The classic exam piece.

10 · Worked Examples & Revision

SOLVED · TOY CAR ON A BEND

0.40 kg toy, radius 0.50 m, 3 revolutions in 10 s → ω = 2π×3/10 = 1.88 rad s⁻¹. v = rω = 0.94 m s⁻¹. F = mrω² = 0.4×0.5×3.55 ≈ 0.71 N.

SOLVED · STONE ON A STRING

0.2 kg stone, vertical circle, r = 0.30 m, string breaks at 8.0 N. At max speed it’s at the bottom: T = mv²/r + mg → 8.0 = 0.2v²/0.3 + 2.0 → v ≈ 3.0 m s⁻¹.

THE METHOD THAT ALWAYS WORKS
  1. Draw the free-body at the point in the question (bottom, top, midpoint).
  2. Identify which direction is “towards the centre” and call it positive.
  3. Write N−mg = mv²/r (or T−mg, N+mg…) with the centreward force as the sum.
  4. Pick v = rω or v = √(ar); never mix v and ω without r.
FORMULA BANK

θ = s/r · ω = Δθ/Δt · ω = 2π/T · v = rω
a = v²/r = rω² · F = mv²/r = mrω²
near-orbit v = √(gr) · bank tanθ = v²/(rg)

COMMON TRAP LIST

No “centrifugal force” on real force diagrams · use radians not degrees in v = rω · pick the correct component (cos for the component to the vertical, sin for the horizontal) · the centre-pointing resultant is constant in size but always changes direction.

Go round gently — every point bends into the centre. ✔
