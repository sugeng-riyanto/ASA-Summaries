EXAMINER'S BRIEF

Everything comes back to four skills: (1) manipulation / measurement, (2) presentation in tables and graphs, (3) analysis, conclusions, evaluation, and (4) planning. The marks live in the small details — column headings with units, decimal places matching, error bars, and a worst-acceptable line that really does pass through every bar.

1 · The Four Measurement Areas

① Manipulation · measurement · observation
Read instruments correctly: rule, protractor, balance, newton meter, meters, measuring cylinder, calipers, micrometer, thermometer, stop-watch.
▼
② Presentation of data
Concise tables with quantity/unit headings; graphs with scaled axes, labelled lines, plotted points.
▼
③ Analysis · conclusions · evaluation
Gradients, weighted lines, percentage differences, limitations & improvements.
▼
④ Planning
Problem → variables · method → apparatus diagrams · analysis → log law · risk assessment.
TECHNIQUE TRAPS
  • ✗ Reading a rule with millimetre scale to more places than possible (never 23.40 cm with a rule) ✔ 23.4 cm, or 23.40 implies 0.1 mm which is wrong.
  • ✗ Averaging the period over one oscillation — always time several (total ≥ 10 s).
  • ✗ not repeating readings → random error stays.

2. Describing a Method

THE 4-STAGE PLAN

1 · Defining the problem

Clearly state the relationship to test. Then identify independent (varied), dependent (measured), and all controlled quantities (kept constant — e.g. same mass & length of a wire).

2. Method

Write so another person could repeat it: precise instruments chosen, how to change independent variable, a labelled diagram (e.g. circuit schematic plus "use extension = L₁ − L₀").

3. Analysis

State directly what graph to plot — usually a straight-line plot and how gradient/intercept gives the constant (write the result explicitly).

4. Safety

What could go wrong and what you'll do — goggles, unplugging the high-voltage, keeping feet clear.

3. Tables, Units & Precision

HEADINGS

Column headings always carry the unit: d/mm, T²/s² — never put units in the body, e.g. write 1.0 then 1.2 …

PRECISION RULE

Raw readings of the same quantity must match precision (same decimal places, matching the instrument). Calculate at least 1 more sf than the least precise input.

ESIMATE OF NUMBER OF POINTS
  • Straight trend → ≥ 6 readings
  • Curved trend → ≥ 9 readings, spread across the full range.

Repeat-averaging reduces random error, measure the curl in steps (e.g. 10, 20, 35, 50, 65, 80 cm).

4. Graphs

PLOTTING RULES
  • Sharp pencil, transparent ruler, points < 1 mm in diameter.
  • Simple scales — the 2 cm square must be worth 1, 2, 5 ×10ⁿ.
  • Points must cover > half of graph grid in both directions. Start not necessarily at origin.
  • Crosses or encircled dots. If a point is anomalous, circle it and label “anomalous” and ignore it.
  • Draw the line of best fit — even distribution of points either side, thickness under 1 mm. Curves by rotating the paper under your hand.
GRADIENT TRAPS

Pick two points on the line, spread apart > half the line length, read coordinates to ≤ 1 mm, and include the powers of ten in the graph labels. Gradient of Δy/Δx, and intercept c = y − mx when the x-axis doesn't start at 0.

5. Straight-line Methods

LINEARISE, THEN SOLVE

For 1/I = (K/E)·L + r/E → plot 1/I vs L: gradient = K/E, intercept = r/E, then K = E × gradient and r = E × intercept.

For a discharge law V = V₀e^(−t/RC) → plot ln V vs t: gradient = −1/RC, intercept = ln V₀, then read RC directly. T² vs m for a mass–spring (gradient = 4π²/k) works the same way — always square a quantity rather than taking square roots by hand.

6. Uncertainties

DEFINITIONS

Absolute (e.g. 1.0 ± 0.1 mm), fractional = absolute/measurement, percentage = fractional × 100%. Repeated readings: absolute uncertainty = half the range (max−min)/2, so mean ± half-range.

COMBINING RULES

＋/−: add absolute uncertainties.
×/÷: add fractional (or %) uncertainties.
Power: multiply fractional by the exponent —
   if x = A·y^a·z^b then Δx/x = a·Δy/y + b·Δz/z (percentages: n × the % of each).

MAX–MIN METHOD

V = L³ with L = 25 ± 1 mm → V_max = 26³ = 17 576, V_min = 24³ = 13 824 → absolute uncertainty ≈ (V_max − V_min)/2 ≈ 1900 ≈ 2000 mm³. Final uncertainties to 1 significant figure (more precise inside a table is OK), final answer as value ± uncertainty with units: (16 000 ± 2000) mm³.

7 · Error Bars & Lines of Best/Worst Fit

PLOTTING ERROR BARS

Draw error bars at every point that has a recorded uncertainty, with a cross tick at each extremity. Draw the best-fit line as a full line, then the worst acceptable — the shallowest or steepest dashed line that still passes through all the error bars (ignoring any anomalous point).

UNCERTAINTY FROM GRAPHS

Δgradient = (steepest − shallowest)/2
Δy-intercept = (steepest − shallowest)/2.

Example (lg T vs lg m): gradient best = 0.50, steepest = 0.536, shallowest = 0.475 → 0.50 ± 0.03; intercept ≈ −0.75 ± 0.07 → constant A = 0.028 ± 0.004.

8 · Testing Relationships with Logarithms

POWER LAW: lg y = lg a + n lg x

Plot lg y vs lg x → straight line. Gradient = n; intercept = lg a ⇒ a = 10^(intercept). Write axes as "lg (m/g)" — the log has no unit, so the unit goes inside the brackets.

EXPONENTIAL: ln y = ln y₀ − bx

Plot ln y against x → straight line. Gradient = −b; intercept = ln y₀ ⇒ y₀ = e^(intercept). Much easier than reading two values off a curve.

SIGNIFICANT FIGURES OF LOGS

Write lg(L/mm) with as many decimal places as sig.figs in the data, e.g. 44.6 cm (3 sf) → lg(L/cm) = 1.649 (3 dp). Note lg(L/cm) not lg L/cm.

9 · Limitations & Improvements

IDENTIFY BY QUANTITY

For each measured quantity think: instrument, difficulty of measuring it, uncertainty. That organises your list.

LIMITATIONS
  • Measuring by eye (judging a bounce, reading the inside of a beaker).
  • Rule held by hand — not vertical / parallax error.
  • Judging start / end of a bounce or swing by eye.
  • Only two readings — k not reliable.
  • Large percentage uncertainty in a small measurement.
  • Objects bouncing to the floor — hazards.
IMPROVEMENTS
  • Clamp stand + set square → vertical rule.
  • Horizontal bar to locate exact starting height; measure ball diameter.
  • Video camera frame-by-frame to catch the bounce top.
    Set-square moved along rule level with the bounce top.
  • Take more readings across range & plot graph (gradient = k).
  • Use a box to catch the ball.

10 · Safety & Planning Review

RISK PATTERN

For each hazard: what is the danger, and what precaution do you take and why it helps —
e.g. goggles when a wire can snap under tension; turn off high-voltage supply when not switching; goggles with lamps.
safety = concern for yourself AND the people around you.

REVIEWING A PLAN

Include an explicit method of working out the analysed constants, a clear diagram, and a statement of what repeats are for. Then re-ask: would another person be able to repeat it to get the same result, and could the uncertainties be reduced?

11 · Key Experiments

MEASURING g

Pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g); measure T (time many swings per L, total ≥ 10 s), plot T² vs L → gradient gives g. Or free-fall with s = ½at², or a ticker/free-fall timer.

YOUNG MODULUS

Wire: measure diameter with a micrometer (at several places along the wire), record extension per load; E = stress/strain = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) → plot F vs ΔL and take the gradient (E = gradient × L/A).

RESISTIVITY

ρ = RA/L: measure diameter (micrometer), length L, and current-and-voltage to get R; plot R vs L → gradient gives ρ/A, so ρ = gradient × A (with A constant).

DOUBLE SLIT / GRATING

Double slit: λ = aΔx/D (fringe separation). Grating: d sin θ = nλ → plot sin θ vs n, gradient = λ/d, hence λ.

INTERNAL RESISTANCE

Classic: V = E − Ir (vary R or the load), plot V vs I → gradient = −r, intercept = E. Also I⁻¹ vs R gives gradient 1/E.

DISCHARGE OF CAPACITOR

RC circuit: plot ln V vs t → gradient = −1/RC, so measure RC; identical shape to radioactive decay (ln N vs t has slope = −λ).

Small details make big marks — measure well, and the marks follow. ✔
